Phase A: Architecture Vision

Core Value Proposition

Sets the aspirational target vision, defines scope, and secures formal mandate to execute[cite: 3, 4].

💡
Must-Know Foundation Fact: Phase A represents the formal start of an architecture development cycle[cite: 3, 4]. It builds directly on the capability established in the Preliminary Phase and begins upon receipt of a formal Request for Architecture Work[cite: 3, 4]. Its core output is an approved Statement of Architecture Work that serves as the governing contract for the project[cite: 3, 4].

Core Purpose

Establish the architecture project, define its boundaries, identify key stakeholders and concerns, and develop a high-level aspirational vision across all BDAT domains (Business, Data, Application, Technology) to solve a business problem and secure executive approval[cite: 3, 4].

Serves as an aspirational vision and boundary for downstream phases[cite: 3, 4].

Phase A Strategic Focus Areas

1. Aspirational Vision

Develop a first-cut, high-level description of Baseline and Target states across all BDAT domains[cite: 3, 4].

2. Project Governance Contract

Consolidate scope, schedule, budget, and responsibilities into an approved Statement of Architecture Work[cite: 3, 4].

3. Business Transformation Readiness

Assess the enterprise's willingness, workforce skills, and cultural readiness to accept change[cite: 3, 4].

4. Scope Definition

Specify what is in-scope and out-of-scope across breadth, depth, time horizon, and architecture levels[cite: 3, 4].

TOGAF 10 Fundamental Content Alignment

In TOGAF 10, Phase A acts as the bridge between enterprise strategy and detailed domain architecture modeling[cite: 3, 4]. By translating high-level business goals and strategic drivers into an overarching Architecture Vision, the architect establishes clear guardrails for subsequent BDAT development in Phases B, C, and D[cite: 3, 4]. It creates the initial draft of the Architecture Definition Document and Architecture Requirements Specification, initializing qualitative and quantitative tracking for the entire ADM cycle[cite: 3, 4].

Branch 1: Triggers & Contractual Deliverables

Phase A is framed by two critical documents that mark the transition into and out of vision development[cite: 3, 4]:

TRIGGERING INPUT Request for Architecture Work

Sent from the sponsoring organization to the architecture team. Triggers Phase A initiation[cite: 3, 4].

GOVERNING CONTRACT Statement of Architecture Work

Approved contract detailing scope, budget, schedule, deliverables, and RACI responsibilities[cite: 3, 4].

Branch 2: 11 Activity Steps Logical Grouping

The 11 detailed steps of Phase A follow a logical progression[cite: 3, 4]:

Initiation (1-3) Establish project, identify stakeholders/concerns, confirm goals & drivers[cite: 3, 4].
Evaluation (4-6) Evaluate capabilities, assess transformation readiness, define scope[cite: 3, 4].
Vision (7-9) Confirm principles, develop Architecture Vision, set value & KPIs[cite: 3, 4].
Approval (10-11) Identify transformation risks, secure approved Statement of Architecture Work[cite: 3, 4].

Branch 3: Qualitative vs. Quantitative Deliverable Containers

Phase A creates draft versions of two companion containers that are refined in subsequent phases[cite: 3, 4]:

Architecture Definition Document (ADD) Provides the QUALITATIVE view of the solution. Contains architecture models across BDAT domains for Baseline, Transition, and Target states[cite: 3, 4].
Architecture Requirements Specification Provides the QUANTITATIVE view of the solution. Contains measurable compliance criteria, service contracts, and performance constraints[cite: 3, 4].

Branch 4: Essential ADM Techniques in Phase A

Two foundational techniques drive information gathering in Phase A[cite: 3, 4]:

Business Scenarios Identifies business needs and derives architecture characteristics. Requires SMART target outcomes (Specific, Measurable, Actionable, Realistic, Time-bound)[cite: 3, 4].
Business Transformation Readiness Assessment Joint effort between HR, corporate staff, and IT planners to evaluate cultural, workforce, and operational readiness to accept change[cite: 3, 4].
⚠️
Exam Pitfall: Remember the sequence! Phase A officially begins upon receipt of a Request for Architecture Work (which is generated in Preliminary or Phase F/H)[cite: 3, 4]. Phase A ends with the approval of the Statement of Architecture Work[cite: 3, 4]. Do not confuse the triggering input document with the final approved contract deliverable!

The 11 Detailed Steps of Phase A[cite: 3, 4]

Click on any step below to view activities, inputs, outputs, and high-yield exam takeaways.

Step 1: Establish the Architecture Project[cite: 3, 4]

Formally set up the architecture project structure, establish project governance mechanisms, allocate architect resources, and initialize tracking steps upon receiving the formal Request for Architecture Work[cite: 3, 4].

  • Review the Request for Architecture Work sent from the sponsoring organization[cite: 3, 4].
  • Conduct preliminary compliance and resource checks[cite: 3, 4].
  • Set up project monitoring tools and repository partitions[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: The Request for Architecture Work is the initiating input created in Preliminary, Phase F, or Phase H[cite: 3, 4].

Step 2: Identify Stakeholders, Concerns, and Business Requirements[cite: 3, 4]

Identify key individuals, teams, or organizations with an interest in the system, document their concerns, and develop a Communications Plan using stakeholder mapping techniques[cite: 3, 4].

  • Analyze stakeholder power, influence, and interest dimensions[cite: 3, 4].
  • Identify key concerns (e.g., security, cost, performance, compliance)[cite: 3, 4].
  • Develop the Communications Plan defining who, what, when, and how information is shared[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: Stakeholders' concerns directly shape the architecture viewpoints selected in downstream phases[cite: 3, 4].

Step 3: Confirm and Elaborate Business Goals, Drivers, and Constraints[cite: 3, 4]

Review the broader enterprise context to confirm business principles, goals, and strategic drivers. Identify enterprise constraints such as budget, timeframes, and regulatory boundaries[cite: 3, 4].

  • Elaborate business strategy and market drivers prompting change[cite: 3, 4].
  • Identify external and internal constraints impacting architecture execution[cite: 3, 4].
  • Re-evaluate refined Statements of Business Principles, Goals, and Drivers[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: Business drivers explain WHY the change is necessary; constraints set strict boundaries on HOW it can be delivered[cite: 3, 4].

Step 4: Evaluate Capabilities[cite: 3, 4]

Assess the baseline business and IT capabilities of the organization to understand current performance levels and identify gaps required to support the proposed transformation[cite: 3, 4].

  • Review baseline Business Capabilities and IT capability maturity[cite: 3, 4].
  • Examine architecture maturity and re-use potential[cite: 3, 4].
  • Document baseline capability gaps in the Capability Assessment deliverable[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: Capability Assessment spans business capabilities, IT function maturity, and EA team capability[cite: 3, 4].

Step 5: Assess Readiness for Business Transformation[cite: 3, 4]

Evaluate the organization's cultural, workforce, and operational readiness to undergo change using Business Transformation Readiness Assessment techniques[cite: 3, 4].

  • Identify transformation readiness factors (e.g., vision, leadership, culture, skills)[cite: 3, 4].
  • Assign maturity ratings and identify readiness risks[cite: 3, 4].
  • Formulate preliminary risk mitigation actions to be incorporated into implementation plans[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: Joint effort between HR, corporate staff, business lines, and IT planners to address human change factors[cite: 3, 4].

Step 6: Define Scope[cite: 3, 4]

Establish what is inside and outside the scope of the architecture effort. Define the breadth, depth, time horizon, and level of the Architecture Landscape to be covered[cite: 3, 4].

  • Define enterprise breadth (affected organizations and business units)[cite: 3, 4].
  • Define architecture domains to be addressed (Business, Data, Application, Technology)[cite: 3, 4].
  • Specify planning horizon (e.g., 3-5 years) and level of detail (Strategic, Segment, or Capability)[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: Scoping prevents "scope creep" and ensures architecture effort matches available budget and authority[cite: 3, 4].

Step 7: Confirm and Elaborate Architecture Principles[cite: 3, 4]

Review, clarify, and elaborate existing Architecture Principles (including Business Principles) established in the Preliminary Phase to ensure alignment with the specific project scope[cite: 3, 4].

  • Verify principles against project goals and resolve ambiguities[cite: 3, 4].
  • Confirm alignment between target architecture direction and principles[cite: 3, 4].
  • Document any necessary principle clarifications or extensions[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: Principles defined in Preliminary are confirmed and clarified in Phase A for project applicability[cite: 3, 4].

Step 8: Develop Architecture Vision[cite: 3, 4]

Formulate a first-cut, high-level aspirational description of the Baseline and Target Architectures across Business, Data, Application, and Technology domains using Business Scenarios[cite: 3, 4].

  • Apply Business Scenarios to uncover core business needs and SMART outcomes[cite: 3, 4].
  • Create high-level architecture views (e.g., Value Chain diagram, Solution Concept diagram)[cite: 3, 4].
  • Draft initial qualitative container (ADD) and quantitative container (Requirements Spec)[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: Creates the Architecture Vision document and initializes the Draft Architecture Definition Document[cite: 3, 4].

Step 9: Define Target Architecture Value Propositions and KPIs[cite: 3, 4]

Outline the direct business value, return on investment, and Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) that the Target Architecture must deliver to satisfy stakeholder expectations[cite: 3, 4].

  • Define business value metrics (e.g., cost reduction, revenue growth, risk mitigation)[cite: 3, 4].
  • Establish quantitative KPIs for measuring solution success[cite: 3, 4].
  • Assess candidate trade-offs and alternative target states[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: Demonstrating business value is critical for securing stakeholder buy-in and funding[cite: 3, 4].

Step 10: Identify Business Transformation Risks and Mitigation[cite: 3, 4]

Uncover potential implementation, cultural, and technical risks associated with the transformation and establish preliminary mitigation plans[cite: 3, 4].

  • Identify initial risk triggers and classify business vs. technical risks[cite: 3, 4].
  • Assess initial risk levels (effect and frequency) prior to mitigation[cite: 3, 4].
  • Formulate mitigation activities to reduce residual risk to acceptable levels[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: Risk management activities initiated in Phase A carry forward into Phases E, F, and G[cite: 3, 4].

Step 11: Develop Statement of Architecture Work and Secure Approval[cite: 3, 4]

Consolidate project scope, workplan, schedule, resource requirements, acceptance criteria, and budget into a formal contract, and obtain formal signature from the sponsoring organization[cite: 3, 4].

  • Draft the formal Statement of Architecture Work based on all Phase A findings[cite: 3, 4].
  • Conduct formal sponsor review and resolve scope or budget trade-offs[cite: 3, 4].
  • Secure formal signature to authorize proceeding to Phase B (Business Architecture)[cite: 3, 4].

🎯 Exam Note: A signed Statement of Architecture Work is the formal contract allowing the team to begin Phase B[cite: 3, 4].

HealthFirst Global Hospital Network: Phase A Execution

Enterprise: 35 Hospitals | 12,000 Staff | 4M Patients Annually

This scenario illustrates how HealthFirst Global executed Phase A (Architecture Vision) to initiate a $45M enterprise transformation migrating legacy hospital Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems to a unified cloud-native Telehealth and Patient Analytics platform[cite: 3, 4].

1. Baseline Problem Statement & Strategic Drivers

Fragmented Legacy Systems

35 regional hospitals operated 14 incompatible EHR software versions, preventing real-time patient record sharing and causing duplicate diagnostic testing.

Compliance & Security Risks

Unencrypted legacy data flows exposed the network to severe HIPAA and GDPR compliance penalties and potential ransomware threats.

Telehealth Channel Demands

Patient demand for remote consultations surged by 300%, but legacy infrastructure could not scale or integrate with mobile health applications.
